\section{Introduction}
A listings site shows thousands of asking prices but gives little help in
reading them. A buyer wants to know whether a flat is priced like comparable
flats, a seller needs a starting price, and the site itself gains from
flagging listings whose price is far from what similar listings ask, whether
through a data-entry error or by choice. All three need a model that maps
what a listing says about the property (its size, type, condition and
location) to a price.

We build such models for bina.az, an Azerbaijani real-estate listings site,
from a public scrape of its sale listings. Task~A regresses
$\log(\text{price})$, a scale on which an error means a relative error and
the few very expensive listings do not dominate. Task~B classifies a listing
as premium or standard, premium meaning a price above the median of the
training listings: the coarser question of which price segment a listing
belongs to. The course requires the main models to be our own NumPy code,
with scikit-learn only as a reference, so the project also tests whether
from-scratch implementations reach library quality on real, messy data.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
  \item a CART decision tree for both tasks, with Gini, entropy and
    squared-error impurity, a vectorised split search, and scikit-learn's
    stopping rules, so that the two trees can be compared node by node;
  \item a soft-margin linear SVM trained with Pegasos, extended with
    mini-batches and suffix averaging, and a study of learning-rate
    schedules;
  \item a non-linear extension of the SVM with random Fourier features that
    approximate an RBF kernel;
  \item a random forest built from our own trees;
  \item a leakage-free pipeline: duplicates and data-entry errors removed by
    fixed rules, every column derived from the price dropped, and the tier
    threshold, the grouping of rare districts and the feature scaling fitted
    on training rows only;
  \item one command, \texttt{python -m src.run\_all}, that reproduces every
    number, table and figure in this report from the raw data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work / Background}
\textbf{Trees and forests.} Hedonic pricing treats the price of a
differentiated good, such as a dwelling, as a function of its
characteristics~\cite{rosen1974hedonic}; this is why most of our features
describe the property and its location. CART
decision trees~\cite{breiman1984cart} fit such a function without assuming
its form. A tree is grown greedily: each node is split on the feature and
threshold that most reduce an impurity measure (the information gain), with
the Gini index or the entropy of the class labels for classification and the
squared error for regression, and each leaf predicts its majority class or
its mean. A deep
tree has low bias but high variance. Random forests~\cite{breiman2001rf}
reduce the variance by averaging many trees, each grown on a bootstrap
sample of the rows and choosing every split among a random subset of the
features.

\textbf{SVMs and Pegasos.} The soft-margin SVM~\cite{cortes1995svm}
separates two classes by a hyperplane with a wide margin and pays a hinge
loss for every point inside the margin or on the wrong side. In its primal
form it minimises $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}$ plus the
mean hinge loss, an objective that is $\lambda$-strongly convex but not
smooth. Pegasos~\cite{pegasos} solves this primal by stochastic
sub-gradient descent on single rows or mini-batches with step
$1/(\lambda t)$, so the cost of a step does not grow with the number of
training rows. For strongly convex problems, averaging only the last part of
the iterates (suffix averaging) attains the optimal $O(1/T)$ rate after $T$
steps, which the average of all iterates can miss by a factor of
$\log T$~\cite{rakhlin2012suffix}. A kernel SVM can fit non-linear
boundaries but needs the $n\times n$ kernel matrix of the training rows.
Random Fourier features~\cite{rahimi2007rff} instead map each row to $D$
random cosine features whose inner products approximate a shift-invariant
kernel such as the RBF kernel, so a linear SVM on the mapped rows
approximates the kernel SVM at the cost of a linear one.
scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} provides the reference models we
compare against (its decision trees, random forests, \texttt{SGDClassifier}
and \texttt{LinearSVC}); none of our own models uses it.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
\textbf{Dataset.} We use the bina.az sale listings published on Kaggle by
\texttt{sehriyarmemmedli} (\texttt{binaaz-sale-project}, file
\texttt{house\_sale.csv}): \val{NRawRows} rows, one per scrape of a listing,
all priced in AZN. Their Azerbaijani columns give the category
(\textit{Kateqoriya}: new build, old stock, house or country house, office,
commercial property, garage, land plot), the area (\textit{Sahə}; in sot for
land plots, 1~sot $=100$~m$^2$), the rooms, the floor (\textit{Mərtəbə},
written ``7 / 9''), the repair status, the ownership-document, mortgage, VIP
and featured flags, the district and the coordinates.

\generatedtable{tables/data.tex}

\textbf{Cleaning.} \texttt{src/data\_prep.py} runs these steps in order;
Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the rows each removes and the final sizes.
(1)~Keep the latest scrape of each listing URL (some prices changed between
scrapes).
(2)~Split the area: a land plot's area is its land area in sot, with floor
area 0; a house's land area has its own column.
(3)~Remove a listing that any of eight fixed, category-aware rules flags:
any listing with a floor area priced below 5{,}000~AZN; a residential listing
(new build, old stock, house) priced below 15{,}000~AZN, under 15~m$^2$, or
with over 300~m$^2$ per room; a commercial property (\textit{Obyekt}) below
100 or above 60{,}000~AZN per m$^2$, and any other listing with a floor area
below 100 or above 15{,}000~AZN per m$^2$; a land plot with a land area
$\le 0$ or above 10{,}000~sot, or below 50~AZN per sot.
(4)~Merge URLs that differ only in format (e.g.\ a \texttt{.html} suffix) by
their numeric listing ID, keeping the latest scrape.
(5)~Remove exact reposts under another ID, keeping the latest: same category,
price, floor and land area, rooms, floor field, exact coordinates and
description. The description keeps genuinely identical flats, e.g.\ in one
new building, apart.
(6)~Fill values missing by design: rooms from the listing's attribute text
(``4 otaqlı''), then 0 for the land area of flats, commercial units and
garages, for a missing floor or number of floors (not recorded for houses,
land, commercial property, offices and garages), and for the rooms of land,
commercial units and garages. Listings whose room count is still unknown are
dropped.
(7)~Drop listings with coordinates outside a bounding box of Azerbaijan
(38.3--42.0$^\circ$N, 44.7--51.0$^\circ$E).
Prices per m$^2$ and per sot exist only inside the outlier rules, which use no
statistics of the data, so applying them before the split leaks nothing. The
total price has no cap: an expensive listing stays if its price per area is
plausible.

\textbf{Features.} The \val{NFeatures} feature columns are eight numeric ones
(floor area, land area, rooms, floor, number of floors, latitude, longitude,
and the great-circle distance in km to the centre of Baku, Fountains Square,
40.37083$^\circ$N, 49.83694$^\circ$E), four binary flags (ownership document,
mortgage, VIP, featured) and one-hot columns for the category, the repair
status (yes, no, unknown) and the district. Districts with fewer than 30
training listings are merged into ``other''; this grouping and the column
layout are learned from the training split only.

\textbf{Excluded columns.} \texttt{unit\_price} and \texttt{total\_price} are
computed from the target (a price per area and a precomputed total), so a
model given either would read off the answer; both are dropped, and the
pipeline asserts that they are absent. Price per m$^2$ is never a feature,
only the trees' Task~A training target (see Experimental Setup). We also
leave out identifiers (owner and shop names, address, URLs, listing ID), the
view count (known only after a listing is published), the English copies of
\textit{Təmir}, \textit{Çıxarış} and \textit{İpoteka}, the building type
(almost always missing), the city (almost always Baku), and the dates, image
URLs and free text.

\textbf{Tier label and split.} A listing is premium if its price is above
the median of the training split, \val{TierThreshold}~AZN, and standard
otherwise; validation and test listings are labelled with this same
threshold. The split is 70/15/15 with seed 42: 30\% of the listings are held
out, stratified by property category (a feature, not the target), the
threshold is computed on the training prices, and the held-out listings are
halved into validation and test, stratified by tier (Fig.~\ref{fig:balance}).
This gives \val{NTrain}, \val{NVal} and \val{NTest} listings, and no listing
ID is in two splits. The SVMs see features standardised with the mean and
standard deviation of the rows they are fitted on (the training split, or the
training folds within cross-validation); the trees use the raw features.
Figure~\ref{fig:price} shows the right-skewed price and its logarithm, the
Task~A target; Figures~\ref{fig:size-price} and~\ref{fig:location} show how
the price varies with size and location.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Decision tree (from scratch)}
Our tree (\texttt{src/decision\_tree.py}, NumPy only) is a binary CART
tree~\cite{breiman1984cart} that sends a row left if $x_f\le\theta$ and right
otherwise, as scikit-learn does. Node impurity is the Gini index
$1-\sum_k p_k^2$ or the entropy $-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k$ of the class shares
$p_k$ (Task~B), or the MSE $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2$ of the node's $N$
targets (Task~A). The best split into $N_L$ and $N_R$ rows maximises the
impurity decrease
\begin{equation}
  \Delta I = I-\tfrac{N_L}{N}\,I_L-\tfrac{N_R}{N}\,I_R .
  \label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}

\textit{Split search.} One stable \texttt{argsort} sorts all candidate columns
at once; cumulative sums of the class indicators, or of the centred targets and
their squares, then give both children's impurity at all $N-1$ split positions
of all features as one array, with no Python loop over thresholds or features.
A position is valid where the sorted value grows by more than $10^{-7}$ (as in
scikit-learn) and both children keep at least min\_samples\_leaf rows; the
threshold is the midpoint of the two values (the lower one if the midpoint
rounds to the upper). Ties go to the lowest feature index, then the
smallest threshold; scikit-learn tries the features in a random order, one
reason the two trees can differ (Table~\ref{tab:tree-study}).

\textit{Stopping and leaves.} A node stays a leaf if it is pure, if it is at
max\_depth, if it has fewer than min\_samples\_split rows (or fewer than twice
min\_samples\_leaf), if no position is valid, or if its best split's
$(N/N_{\text{train}})\,\Delta I$ is below min\_impurity\_decrease, the
weighting scikit-learn uses. A leaf stores its rows' class frequencies, which
\texttt{predict\_proba} returns, or their mean target; \texttt{predict}
returns the most frequent class (the lower label on a tie) or the mean.
\texttt{feature\_importances\_} sums $N\,\Delta I$ over each feature's splits,
normalised to one. We tune max\_depth, min\_samples\_leaf and the Task~B
criterion; min\_samples\_split stays at 2 and min\_impurity\_decrease at~0.

\textit{Random forest (bonus).} \texttt{src/random\_forest.py} grows 100 of
our trees~\cite{breiman2001rf} with no depth limit, each on a bootstrap sample of
the $n$ training rows; at each node a tree searches only the first $k$
features of a random permutation that vary in the node
($k=\lfloor\sqrt{d}\rfloor$ for Task~B, $\lfloor d/3\rfloor$ for Task~A; leaf
sizes in Table~\ref{tab:tree-study}). Task~B averages the trees' class
probabilities and takes the most probable class (soft voting); Task~A averages
their predictions.

\subsection{Support-vector machine (from scratch)}
Our SVM (\texttt{src/svm.py}) is a linear soft-margin
SVM~\cite{cortes1995svm} trained in the primal with Pegasos~\cite{pegasos}.
With labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ (premium $=+1$) it minimises
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  J(\mathbf{w},b)={}&\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}+b^{2}\big)\\
  &+\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\!\big(0,\,1-y_i(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i+b)\big).
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:svm-objective}
\end{equation}
The bias is regularised like the weights, as in liblinear, which appends a
constant feature. Eq.~\eqref{eq:svm-objective} is then exactly the objective
that scikit-learn's \texttt{LinearSVC}~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} minimises
with the hinge loss, $C=1/(\lambda n)$ and \texttt{intercept\_scaling}${}=1$,
so its $J$ is a reference optimum.

Each step $t$ draws a mini-batch $B_t$ of $B$ rows uniformly at random and,
with $A_t=\{i\in B_t: y_i(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i+b)<1\}$ the rows whose
hinge is active, updates
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{w}&\leftarrow(1-\eta_t\lambda)\,\mathbf{w}
    +\tfrac{\eta_t}{B}\textstyle\sum_{i\in A_t}y_i\mathbf{x}_i,\\
  b&\leftarrow(1-\eta_t\lambda)\,b
    +\tfrac{\eta_t}{B}\textstyle\sum_{i\in A_t}y_i .
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:pegasos-step}
\end{equation}
With $B=1$ this is the original Pegasos step; an epoch is $\lceil n/B\rceil$
steps. The step size is $\eta_t=1/(\lambda t)$ (\emph{pegasos}, the default),
$1/(\lambda(t+t_0))$ with $t_0=1/(\lambda\eta_0)$ (\emph{offset}: the steps
start near $\eta_0$ instead of at $1/\lambda$), or $\eta_0$ (\emph{constant}).
The SVM study and both final SVMs use \emph{offset} with
$\eta_0=1/\overline{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}$, one over the mean squared
norm of the training rows: one single-row step of that size moves the margin
$y_if(\mathbf{x}_i)$ of a row of average norm by about one, whatever the
feature scale. The first step of $1/(\lambda t)$ is $1/\lambda$ instead, $10^{4}$
at $\lambda=10^{-4}$; it throws $\mathbf{w}$ far from the optimum, and at a
small $\lambda$ the shrinking steps take many epochs to bring it back
(Table~\ref{tab:svm-convergence}).
With suffix averaging~\cite{rakhlin2012suffix}, \texttt{fit} returns the mean
of the iterates over the second half of the $T$ steps
($t>\lfloor T/2\rfloor$). \texttt{history\_} records $J$ on all training rows
once per epoch, for the weights \texttt{fit} would return at that point.

After fitting, the model reports $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, the margin width
$2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ between the hyperplanes
$f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b=\pm1$ in standardised feature
units ($b$ is not part of the geometric margin), and the support vectors, the
training rows with $y_if(\mathbf{x}_i)\le1$ (on or inside the margin,
misclassified rows included), as a count and a share. Features are
standardised with the fit rows' mean and standard deviation
(\texttt{baselines.StandardScaled}), per CV fold.

\textit{Non-linear extension.} \texttt{src/rff.py} (NumPy only) maps a row to
$D$ random Fourier features~\cite{rahimi2007rff},
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{2/D}\,\cos\!\big(W^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}\big),
  \label{eq:rff}
\end{equation}
with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}$, $W_{jl}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2\gamma)$ and
$c_l\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)$, so that
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x}')]
=\exp(-\gamma\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^{2})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$,
the RBF kernel; linear Pegasos then runs on $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$, with
$f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})+b$. We measure the
approximation by the mean of
$|\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x}')-k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')|$
over all pairs of 500 training rows. Kernelised Pegasos would keep one coefficient
per training row and evaluate the kernel against the support set at every
step and every prediction, $O(nd)$ work each, far slower for \val{NTrain}
rows; random features cost $O(Dd)$ per row, keep the solver linear and $J$
cheap to evaluate, so the same convergence checks apply.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Protocol.} Every model sees the same 70/15/15 training, validation
and test split (seed 42). The trees' settings are chosen on the validation
split only (Table~\ref{tab:tree-study}), and so are the SVMs': the SVM study
(\texttt{src/svm\_study.py}, Table~\ref{tab:svm-study}) sees only the training
and validation splits and fixes, for our linear SVM, $\lambda={}$\val{SvmLambda}
and \val{SvmEpochs} epochs of mini-batches of \val{SvmBatch} rows with the
offset schedule and suffix averaging, and for our RFF SVM $\gamma={}$\val{RffGamma},
$\lambda={}$\val{RffLambda} and $D={}$\val{RffD}; the table lists these
and the remaining solver settings. Their scikit-learn counterparts get the
same settings. The Task~B threshold is the
training median price, and the test split is scored once, with the final
settings. Each model is also cross-validated on the training split (5 folds,
stratified by tier for Task~B; mean $\pm$ std), and each headline test metric
has a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval (1{,}000 resamples of the test rows).

\textbf{Metrics.} Task~A: RMSE, MAE and $R^2$ on $\log(\text{price})$, the
scale the models are trained on, and the median absolute percentage error of
the price in AZN, which is easier to read. Task~B: precision, recall and F1
for the premium class, accuracy, ROC-AUC from each model's continuous scores,
and the confusion matrix.

\textbf{Task~A target for the trees.} A tree can only follow the floor area
in steps, so both trees are fitted on $\log(\text{price}/\text{area})$ (area
in m$^2$, or in sot for land plots) and the listing's own $\log(\text{area})$
is added back to the prediction. The area is an ordinary feature of the
listing; \texttt{unit\_price} and \texttt{total\_price} are dropped from
every feature matrix and price per m$^2$ is never an input, only the training
target. Because the area is added back exactly, the residuals are the same as
on $\log(\text{price})$, so every Task~A metric, $R^2$ included, is computed
on $\log(\text{price})$. The tree settings are tuned on the validation split
(\texttt{src/tree\_experiments.py}, Table~\ref{tab:tree-study}) and the same
settings are used in every table.

\textbf{Baselines.} scikit-learn's~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}
\texttt{DecisionTreeRegressor} and \texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier} get our
tree's criterion, depth and leaf settings. Against our linear SVM we fit
\texttt{SGDClassifier} (hinge loss, $\alpha=\lambda$, its default step
$1/(\alpha(t+t_0))$, which has the form of our offset schedule, the same
number of epochs, and averaging over the second half of the updates) and \texttt{LinearSVC}
(hinge loss, $C=1/(\lambda n)$ for $n$ fit rows, so that it minimises our
$J$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:svm-objective}). Against our RFF SVM we fit
\texttt{RBFSampler} with the same $\gamma$ and $D$ followed by
\texttt{SGDClassifier} (hinge loss, $\alpha$ equal to the RFF SVM's
$\lambda$). Each runs in a \texttt{Pipeline} after \texttt{StandardScaler}.
All of them target the same $\lambda$-regularised hinge loss on features
standardised with the fit rows' statistics (so each CV fold is scaled with its
own training rows), but none is run to the exact
optimum: ours and SGD stop after a fixed number of epochs, and
\texttt{LinearSVC} at its tolerance or after 20{,}000 iterations. They also
differ in the details: SGD updates on one row at a time, so the same number of
epochs means about $B$ times as many updates as ours, and it leaves its intercept
unregularised; \texttt{RBFSampler} draws its own $W$ and $\mathbf{c}$, so the
two RFF models do not share their random features. The convergence study
measures how far our solver is from \texttt{LinearSVC}'s objective
(Table~\ref{tab:svm-convergence}). Trivial floors predict the training mean
(Task~A) and the majority class (Task~B).

\textbf{Analysis and timing.} Permutation importance (on the validation
split, for every model) and the trees' impurity decrease treat all one-hot
columns of a feature as one; for the linear SVMs we show the largest
individual weights on the standardised columns. Test errors are broken down
by fixed price bands, property category, the number of training listings in
the listing's district and the distance from the tier threshold, and we list
the listings our tree misprices most.
Fit and predict times are wall-clock seconds on one machine; fit time against
training-set size is the median of three fits on nested subsamples of
$1/8$, $1/4$, $1/2$ and all of the training split.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All randomness is seeded, and
\texttt{python -m src.run\_all} regenerates every number, table and figure
in this report from the raw data; a test checks that two runs give identical
numbers apart from the timings.

The SVM study (\texttt{src/svm\_study.py}) chooses the settings of both our
SVMs on the validation split and never sees the test split;
Table~\ref{tab:svm-study} lists the chosen settings and their scores, and the
headline Task~B models use them. The study has four parts.
(1)~It sweeps $\lambda$ over the eleven values
$\{10^{-6},3\cdot10^{-6},\dots,10^{-1}\}$, each with 50 epochs of mini-batches
of 32 rows, the offset schedule and suffix averaging. It records validation
F1 and AUC, training F1, $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, the margin
$2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, the share of support vectors and $J$, and keeps
the $\lambda$ with the highest validation F1, the larger one (the wider
margin) on a tie (Figure~\ref{fig:svm-lambda}). This gives
$\lambda={}$\val{SvmLambda}.
(2)~At that $\lambda$ it measures how close our solver gets to the optimum,
as the gap $J/J_{\text{LinearSVC}}-1$ to \texttt{LinearSVC}'s objective at the
same $\lambda$ (Table~\ref{tab:svm-convergence},
Figure~\ref{fig:svm-objective}). The setting \texttt{run\_all} used before
($B=1$, 200{,}000 steps of $1/(\lambda t)$, the last iterate) ends with a gap
of \val{PegasosOldObjGapPct}\%. The study then runs mini-batches of 32 rows
for 50 epochs without averaging, and with averaging for 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100
epochs, and keeps the fewest epochs whose $J$ is within 1\% of
\texttt{LinearSVC}'s (or the most, if none is): here \val{SvmEpochs} epochs,
with a gap of \val{SvmObjGapPct}\%. With these settings the margin is
\val{SvmMargin} wide in standardised feature units, and \val{SvmSupportPct}\%
of the training rows are support vectors.
(3)~At the chosen $\lambda$ and number of epochs it runs the three
learning-rate schedules (\emph{offset} and \emph{constant} with
$\eta_0={}$\val{SvmEtaZero}) and records $J$, the margin and validation F1,
to see how much the schedule alone changes where the solver ends
(Figure~\ref{fig:svm-objective}).
(4)~For the RFF SVM it searches
$\gamma\in\{3\cdot10^{-4},10^{-3},3\cdot10^{-3},10^{-2},3\cdot10^{-2}\}$
$\times$ $\lambda\in\{10^{-6},10^{-5},10^{-4}\}$ with $D=1024$ random
features, at the chosen number of epochs, by validation F1 (ties: the larger
$\lambda$, then the smaller $\gamma$). This gives $\gamma={}$\val{RffGamma} and
$\lambda={}$\val{RffLambda}. As a check of the approximation, it then varies
$D\in\{32,128,512,1024,2048\}$ at those values and records validation F1 and
AUC and the kernel approximation error (Table~\ref{tab:svm-rff},
Figure~\ref{fig:svm-rff}); $D$ itself is not tuned. The headline RFF SVM uses
$D={}$\val{RffD}, where the mean approximation error is \val{RffApproxErr}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Task~A.} \textbf{[TO FILL after the full run: our tree against
scikit-learn's tree, our forest and the training mean
(Table~\ref{tab:task-a}); in which price bands and districts the errors are
largest (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors-a}, Table~\ref{tab:errors}); what the listings
in Table~\ref{tab:worst} have in common; and the bias--variance reading of
Fig.~\ref{fig:tree-study} behind the chosen depth \val{TreeADepth} and leaf
size \val{TreeALeaf}.]}

\textbf{Task~B.} \textbf{[TO FILL after the full run: which model wins
Task~B and by how much (F1 and AUC with their intervals,
Table~\ref{tab:task-b}), and whether the random-feature SVM (F1
\val{RffBFOne}, AUC \val{RffBAuc}) beats the linear one (F1 \val{SvmBFOne},
AUC \val{SvmBAuc}) by more than the intervals.]} A linear SVM gives each
feature one weight, the same in every district, so a square metre moves the
margin by the same amount in the centre and on the outskirts. The trees and
the random-feature SVM can model such interactions, so their gap to the
linear SVM indicates how much the interactions matter for the tier. Our
Task~B tree uses the \val{TreeBCriterion} criterion, a maximum depth of
\val{TreeBDepth} and leaves of at least \val{TreeBLeaf} listings, and reaches
an AUC of \val{TreeBAuc}.

\textbf{Against scikit-learn.} With the same settings, our tree makes the
same test prediction as scikit-learn's on a share of \val{TreeAgreeA} of the
listings for Task~A and \val{TreeAgreeB} for Task~B. Both are CART on the same
rows, so the differences can only come from ties between equally good splits
and from where a threshold sits between two feature values. Our SVM ends
\val{SvmObjGapPct}\% from \texttt{LinearSVC}'s objective, against
\val{PegasosOldObjGapPct}\% for single-row Pegasos with step $1/(\lambda t)$
and no averaging, and \val{SvmSupportPct}\% of the training listings are
support vectors. \textbf{[TO FILL after the full run: what the support share
says about how much the two tiers overlap; the speed comparison from
Table~\ref{tab:timing} and Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}.]}

\textbf{Limitations.} \textit{Near-duplicates across splits.} The repost
filter requires an identical description, so a property posted again with new
text passes it. \val{NCrossSplitDup} of the validation and test listings
(\val{PctCrossSplitDup}\%) have a twin in another split with the same
category, price, floor and land area, rooms, floor field and exact
coordinates, but a different listing ID and description: almost certainly the
same property. A twin across validation and test means that settings tuned on
validation were partly tuned on test listings, so tuned models may look
slightly better on test than they should; a twin in the training split makes
that test listing easier for every model. A looser key would also merge
genuinely identical flats in one new building, so we keep the strict one.

\textit{Asking prices.} The prices are what sellers ask, not what buyers pay:
a listing the models call overpriced is overpriced relative to other asking
prices, and we cannot measure the gap to transaction prices.

\textit{One snapshot.} The data are one scrape of one site with no time
split, so we cannot say how quickly the models go out of date as prices move.

\textit{One threshold.} Premium is a single cut at the training median, so
two listings a few per cent apart in price can get different labels with
nothing else to separate them. If the errors concentrate in the bands
closest to the threshold, part of the Task~B error comes from the label
itself, not from the models; Fig.~\ref{fig:errors-b} gives the error rate by
distance from the threshold. \textbf{[TO FILL after the full run: say whether
the figure shows this.]}

\textit{Model classes.} Our SVMs are either linear in the standardised
features or use \val{RffD} random features that approximate one RBF kernel
(mean approximation error \val{RffApproxErr}), with a single bandwidth $\gamma$ of
\val{RffGamma} for all standardised columns, one-hot columns included.
Neither is an exact kernel SVM, which would need the kernel matrix of all
\val{NTrain} training listings.

\textit{Timings.} Fit and predict times are wall-clock seconds on one
machine, the full fits are single runs, and they depend on the hardware and
the linear-algebra library. Our models loop over nodes or steps in Python
while scikit-learn's are compiled, so the timings compare implementations
more than algorithms.

\section{Conclusion}
We built a CART decision tree, a Pegasos SVM with mini-batches, suffix
averaging and random Fourier features, and a random forest of our own trees
in NumPy, and evaluated them on bina.az sale listings against scikit-learn
models with matched settings on one leakage-free split.
\textbf{[TO FILL after the full run: the best model on each task and its test
score, how close our implementations come to scikit-learn's, and where the
errors concentrate.]} Next we would split the data by groups of listings that
share category, price, areas and coordinates, so that no repost can land in
two splits; use a time-based split once a later scrape is available; and
predict the tier from the Task~A price prediction, to see whether a separate
classifier is needed at all.
